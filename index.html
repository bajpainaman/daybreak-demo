the gap

AI can compute.
It can't prove it's right.

Models generate code, answers, whole programs — and we check them by vibes. At the scale AI now writes software, "looks correct" is not a foundation. One wrong register, one skipped validation, one silent overflow, and trust is gone.

The frontier is racing to make models that write more. Daybreak makes computation you can prove.

One loop under everything we built. The model proposes; an exact checker — a compiler, a test, a proof, an independent twin — decides. The verifier isn't just the judge. It's also the teacher and the data source.

proof iii · the verifier trains the model

The verifier
is the teacher.

A model from scratch — no pretrained weights — learns to synthesize programs by inverting the exact interpreter: every random program is a free, perfectly-labeled example. Pure reinforcement from reward collapsed (0/10, worse than random). This bootstraps immediately. And its output isn't a toy format — it's real MLIR the actual compiler accepts and runs.

The space explodes.

As programs get harder, the search space grows exponentially — 13 orders of magnitude across these lengths. Blind search drowns. The verifier-trained model's cost to a proven-correct program stays ~5 candidates and solves 18/20 throughout.

Not "bigger model, more text." Learned proposal makes an exponentially-growing space navigable at flat cost — the edge compounds as problems get harder. The same loop scales to MLIR: programs become IR edits, the verifier becomes compile-and-test over a whole codebase's history.

the honest part

What didn't work.

  • Representation isn't the lever. The same anomaly scan localizes bugs no better on IR than on source (6.1% vs 7.0%); library LLVM IR can't even see generic functions. We learned it for ~$3 instead of betting a raise on it.
  • Pure RL from reward collapses from scratch — it never finds the first correct answer to learn from. The fix: let the verifier generate the data, not just score it.
  • Both failures shaped the architecture that works. We can afford to report them — because the verifier makes being wrong cheap.
